## Understanding the FAIR Principles

**Designed for Researchers and Principal Investigators (PIs) Across All Disciplines**

## Introduction

Research today is characterised by increasing digital complexity, interdisciplinary collaborations, and expectations of integrity, transparency, and responsible stewardship. The **FAIR principles Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable** provide a coherent and flexible framework for ensuring that research data, metadata, and outputs are well-managed, shareable, and usable by both humans and machines.

Although originally developed for scientific data management and stewardship, the FAIR Principles were intentionally designed to apply broadly to digital objects across disciplines, including data, metadata, software, workflows, and other research outputs. The principles focus on making digital resources Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable for both humans and machines. [Go-Fair](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/), [MIT](https://direct.mit.edu/dint/article/2/1-2/10/10017/FAIR-Principles-Interpretations-and-Implementation)

According to [Go-Fair](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/), FAIR principles can therefore be adapted to virtually any research domain where data or digital outputs are created, including:

- **Laboratory Research** assigning persistent identifiers (e.g., DOIs) to datasets, documenting experimental protocols, and depositing data in searchable repositories.
- **Clinical Studies** using standard metadata, controlled vocabularies, and secure access procedures so sensitive data remain accessible under appropriate authorisation. 
- **Social Science Research** publishing survey data with rich metadata, clear licences, and documentation that enables future reuse and replication. 
- **Archaeology** describing artefacts, excavation records, and spatial data using community standards to support discovery and interoperability across collections. 
- **Linguistics** sharing annotated corpora and language resources with persistent identifiers and machine-readable metadata. 
- **Literary and Digital Humanities Research** publishing digitised texts, annotations, and analytical datasets using standard formats and - reusable metadata. 
- **Computational Research** making code, workflows, and associated datasets discoverable, documented, and reusable through repositories and version control systems. 
- **Cross-disciplinary Research** enabling data integration across domains through shared standards, vocabularies, and interoperable metadata. 

The FAIR Principles are intentionally **discipline-agnostic**, they do not prescribe specific technologies or standards but instead provide high-level guidance that communities can implement according to their own disciplinary needs and practices. FAIR is not a rigid checklist it is a set of guiding principles designed to support good data stewardship across diverse research environments.


## What the FAIR Principles Are and How They Operate

The FAIR principles were introduced in 2016 in response to the growing complexity, volume, and diversity of research data. They are **guiding principles**, not strict rules, designed to promote responsible and effective data management.

A dataset created by:

- A biomedical laboratory  
- A social science research team  
- A historian working with medieval manuscripts  

will look very different. Yet all can become more intelligible, discoverable, and reusable through FAIR practice.

FAIR provides a shared language for good data practice across research cultures.

### Across Disciplines

- **Health and biosciences** : Supports clinical reproducibility, secure data sharing, and compliance with governance frameworks.
- **STEM fields**: Encourages structured metadata, persistent identifiers, and computational interoperability.
- **Humanities**: Supports rich contextual metadata, detailed source description, and ethical handling of cultural materials.
- **Social sciences**: Strengthens transparency, enhances discoverability of qualitative and quantitative data, and promotes responsible reuse under appropriate licences.

> **Important:** FAIR is *not* synonymous with open.  
> Data can be FAIR and closed, provided access conditions and reuse terms are clearly described.

## Why FAIR Matters

The fundamental purpose of FAIR is to ensure that research data and metadata remain:

- Meaningful  
- Discoverable  
- Interpretable  
- Usable  

long beyond the lifetime of a single project.

FAIR enables data to be understood not only by future human researchers but also by machines. This is increasingly critical in research environments reliant on:

- Automation  
- Large-scale analytics  
- Machine learning  
- Artificial intelligence  

FAIR operates as a coherent ecosystem, where each component reinforces the others.
