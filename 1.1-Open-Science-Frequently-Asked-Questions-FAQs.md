# Open Science Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

## What is the difference between Open Access and Open Science?

There are similarities in that both models aim to make research freely available.

**Open Access** refers specifically to making research publications freely accessible. There are two main routes:

- **Gold route**  publication is made openly available via the publishers website.
- **Green route**  publication is deposited in an institutional repository such as:
  - [Pure](https://www.elsevier.com/en-gb/products/pure)
  - [DSpace](https://dspace.org/)

Work can also be shared via blogs or other free platforms, although these may not be suitable if citation tracking and research impact are priorities.

**Open Science**, by contrast, is a broader philosophy. It seeks to make the *entire research process* transparent  including datasets, methodologies, workflows, and protocols  enabling collaboration, verification, and reproducibility.

## Why is Open Science important?

To understand its importance, we should ask: *Why do we do research?*

For most researchers, the answer lies in the impact of sharing knowledge. Open Science removes barriers to access by making methods and results openly available so others can:

- Verify findings  
- Detect bias or errors  
- Reproduce studies  
- Build upon existing work  

This approach saves time, enhances collaboration across institutions, and fosters innovation.

A clear example is the COVID-19 pandemic, during which researchers shared data globally, accelerating vaccine development.

Open Science also supports **citizen science**, extending participation beyond academia. Examples include:

- [National Geographic  Citizen Science Overview](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/citizen-science-article/)
- [Zooniverse](https://www.zooniverse.org/)  a platform connecting researchers with volunteers worldwide.


## Does Open Science only include scientific research?

The term can be misleading. Historically, science referred primarily to natural sciences. However, Open Science applies broadly across disciplines and is sometimes described as **Open Scholarship**.

UNESCO defines science as systematic scholarly knowledge production:

- [An Introduction to the UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science (PDF)](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/1825/an_introduction_to_the_unesco_recommendation_on_open_science.pdf)

Open Science can apply to:

- A philosopher publishing an open access article  
- An economist sharing a dataset  
- An educator publishing case studies  
- An artist sharing an open learning experience  

## Are qualitative and interpretivist studies part of Open Science?

Yes.

Open Science principles of transparency and accessibility apply equally to interpretivist research. Even when methodologies include ethnography, interviews, or discourse analysis, transparency can be enhanced by sharing:

- Interview guides  
- Methodological decisions  
- Coding frameworks  
- Reflexive positionality statements  

This strengthens rigour and reproducibility, even in qualitative research.


## Where are Open Science resources stored?

Resources are stored in accessible and reusable repositories. The location depends on what is being shared.

Examples include:

- Institutional repositories (for pre-prints and accepted manuscripts)
- Subject-specific repositories such as:
  - [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/) (Philosophy)
  - [SSRN - Social Sciences Research Network](https://www.ssrn.com/ssrn/)


## What is the difference between open repositories and research networking sites such as ResearchGate?

**ResearchGate** is a commercial social networking platform for researchers. It allows users to share publications and interact with peers.

Open repositories, however, are designed specifically for preservation, accessibility, and reproducibility. Open Science is not just about sharing final publications  it encompasses the entire research lifecycle.


## Does it cost anything to make my work freely available?

Open Science *can* involve costs, but it does not have to.

- Some journals charge Article Processing Charges (APCs) for Open Access.
- Depositing work in institutional repositories is usually free.
- Free repositories include:
  - [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/)
  - [OSF - Open Science Framework](https://osf.io/)

Some data repositories charge fees for hosting large datasets. However, sharing code on platforms like [GitHub](https://github.com/) is free (aside from your time).


## What does FAIR data mean and why should we follow these principles?

**FAIR** stands for:

- **Findable**
- **Accessible**
- **Interoperable**
- **Reusable**

The FAIR framework ensures data is well-managed and usable by both humans and machines. These are guiding principles that promote good data stewardship rather than rigid rules.

For more detail, see [3.1. Understanding the FAIR Principles](https://github.com/Digital-Future-Institute/CoARA/blob/main/3.1.-Understanding-the-FAIR-Principles.md)

## How is intellectual copyright protected?

Open Science promotes collaboration and transparency  but researchers retain their intellectual rights.

Copyright is automatically granted to the creator. Open Science uses **open licences** to define how others may use the work.

These licences allow you to specify:

- Whether attribution is required  
- Whether commercial use is allowed  
- Whether adaptations are permitted  

Common licence types include:

- Creative Commons licences  
- Open Source licences  
- Open Data licences  

To learn more [about licenses, see](https://github.com/Digital-Future-Institute/CoARA/blob/main/2.-Open-Licensing-in-Open-Science.md)  



## Where do I start?

Getting started can be gradual and manageable. Some practical first steps:

- Learn about your institutions repository and submission process  
- Share a pre-print  
- Share a research protocol  
- Read about open licensing and apply a licence to your next project  
- Explore repositories such as Zenodo or OSF  
- Join an Open Science community (local university groups, library-led networks, or national initiatives such as Open Research UK or OpenAIRE)  
- Talk to colleagues about Open Science  
- Share these FAQs
