## What is a Data Management Plan?

A **Data Management Plan (DMP)** is a formal document that describes how research data will be handled, **both during a project and after it concludes**. It covers how data will be **collected, organised, stored, shared, and preserved**. DMPs are increasingly required by research funders and institutions, but even when not required, they are a best practice for responsible research.

## Why Are DMPs Important?

- **Organisation:** Planning data management in advance helps prevent confusion and data loss.  
- **Compliance:** Many funders and journals require DMPs to ensure data is handled ethically and remains accessible.  
- **Open Science:** DMPs support transparency, reproducibility, and data sharing, which are core principles of Open Science.  
- **Efficiency:** A clear plan saves time and resources during the research process.  


## Key Elements of a Data Management Plan

### 1. Description of Datasets

Describing your datasets is fundamental to your DMP. It helps others (and your future self) understand what data you are working with, how it is created, and where it comes from. A clear description ensures your data can be interpreted, reused, and cited correctly.

**Points to address:**

- **Types of data:** Quantitative (numbers, measurements), qualitative (text, interviews), or both.  
- **Source:** Primary data (collected directly), secondary data (from existing sources), or simulated/generated data.  
- **Generation methods:** Outline methods or processes used to collect or create the data.  

**Examples:**

- **Survey Data:** Responses from ~200 undergraduate students via online questionnaire on climate change, including multiple-choice and open-ended questions.  
- **Experimental Data:** Soil pH measurements collected from various sites using a calibrated pH meter.  
- **Simulated Data:** Climate data generated using the WRF model for the East of England region over 10 years.  
- **Compiled Data:** Aggregated public census data combined with local authority records.  


### 2. Types of Datasets

Understanding the type of dataset helps plan how it will be managed and shared:

- **Observational:** Collected by observing natural phenomena. *Example:* Field notes from bird behaviour.  
- **Experimental:** Generated through controlled experiments. *Example:* Laboratory tests measuring drug effectiveness.  
- **Simulated:** Produced by models or simulations. *Example:* Synthetic weather data for climate predictions.  
- **Compiled:** Aggregated from multiple sources. *Example:* Database combining government statistics.  


### 3. Forms of Data

Datasets can take many forms, such as:

- **Text:** Interview transcripts, field notes, open-ended survey responses.  
- **Numeric:** Measurements, counts, survey scores, spreadsheets or databases.  
- **Audiovisual:** Audio recordings, photographs, videos.  
- **Code:** Scripts, algorithms, models (Python, R, etc.).  
- **Instrument-specific:** Data outputs from specialised equipment, e.g., chromatograms, microscope images.  

**Why this matters:** Clear dataset descriptions ensure transparency, reproducibility, and compliance with funders and publishers.


## Naming and Referencing Datasets

Clear and consistent **naming and referencing** ensures datasets can be identified, retrieved, cited, and reused effectively.

### Naming Conventions

A structured approach helps avoid confusion, supports version control, and facilitates collaboration.

**Guidelines:**

- **Descriptive:** Include project name, location, date, and data type.  
- **Consistent:** Use the same format throughout the project.  
- **Readable:** Avoid overly complex abbreviations.  
- **Machine-friendly:** Use underscores (_) or hyphens (-); avoid special characters.  

**Examples:**

- `2025_UK_SoilpH_Survey.csv` Soil pH survey data, UK, 2025  
- `InterviewTranscripts_Phase1.txt` Interview transcripts, first phase  
- `Exp3_TemperatureReadings_15May2025.xlsx` Temperature readings from Experiment 3 on 15 May 2025  

**Versioning:** Include version numbers (e.g., `SurveyData_v1.csv`, `SurveyData_v2.csv`).  
**Date format:** Use ISO 8601 (YYYY-MM-DD), e.g., `2025-05-15`.  

### Persistent Identifiers (PIDs)

A **Persistent Identifier** is a stable, unique reference to a digital object. The most common PID for datasets is the **Digital Object Identifier (DOI)**.  

- **Best Practices:**
  - **Plan early:** Establish naming conventions at project start and document them in your DMP.  
  - **Be consistent:** Apply rules across all datasets and files.  
  - **Use PIDs:** Assign persistent identifiers to all final/published datasets.  
  - **Document versions:** Track dataset versions and changes over time.
