## Introduction

Since the 1990s, researchers in the humanities and social sciences have increasingly recognised that place matters. The *spatial turn* in scholarship has highlighted how geography, location, movement, and landscape shape cultural production, social relationships, historical events, and human experiences.

While humanities research has traditionally focused on texts, objects, archives, and narratives, geo-spatial approaches allow researchers to examine how these materials are connected to places and spaces. Spatial methods can reveal patterns of movement, relationships between communities, changes in landscapes, and the cultural significance of particular locations. By combining geographical information with qualitative and quantitative evidence, researchers can uncover new perspectives that might not emerge through textual analysis alone.

Geo-spatial research is now used across a wide range of disciplines, including:

- History
- Archaeology
- Literary Studies
- Heritage and Museum Studies
- Anthropology
- Human Geography
- Sociology
- Political Science
- Linguistics
- Education Studies
- Migration Studies

This guide introduces the practical use of geo-spatial methods in humanities and social sciences research and provides an overview of how geo-spatial data can be created, documented, analysed, and shared openly.

## What is Geo-Spatial Humanities Research?

Geo-spatial humanities research examines how people, ideas, cultural artefacts, events, and stories relate to place.

Rather than asking only:

> **What happened?**  
> **What does a text mean?**

Spatial inquiry also asks:

> **Where did it happen?**  
> **How did people move through space?**  
> **How does place influence experience, identity, and memory?**  
> **How are social and cultural processes distributed across landscapes?**

By focusing on the relationships between people and places, geo-spatial humanities research explores how geographical contexts shape cultural, historical, and social phenomena. It allows researchers to investigate not only events themselves but also the spatial dimensions that influence how those events unfold and are experienced.

Spatial methods often involve:

- Digital maps
- Geographic Information Systems (GIS)
- Location-based datasets
- Spatial databases
- Interactive visualisations
- Georeferenced historical sources
- Mapping and spatial analysis tools

However, the goal is not simply to produce maps. The purpose of geo-spatial humanities research is to generate new forms of interpretation and understanding. By analysing spatial patterns, relationships, and movements, researchers can uncover connections and insights that may not be visible through textual, visual, or statistical analysis alone.

## When Can Humanities and Social Sciences Research Benefit from Geo-Spatial Inquiry?

Geo-spatial approaches are particularly useful when research questions involve place, movement, territory, landscape, memory, networks, or spatial relationships. By incorporating geographical perspectives into research design, scholars can explore how people, ideas, and cultural practices are shaped by their spatial contexts.

### Locating Literary Landscapes and Textual Topography

Literary works frequently contain rich geographical information. Mapping places mentioned in texts can help researchers explore how authors represent landscapes, cities, borders, and identities.


Researchers might extract references to streets, neighbourhoods, and landmarks from Dickens' novels and visualise them using GIS software. Mapping these references may reveal how poverty, industrialisation, and social class were represented spatially across Victorian London. 

**Example: Literary Journeys** [Mapping Dickens' London: Charles Dickens London Map](https://www.charlesdickenspage.com/charles-dickens-london-map.html)

Travel writing, diaries, and novels often contain journeys that can be reconstructed geographically.

Researchers might map:

- Pilgrimage routes
- Colonial expeditions
- Travel narratives
- Fictional journeys

By visualising movement across space, researchers can better understand patterns of mobility, encounter, displacement, and cultural exchange. See:
- [UT Austin GIS for Digital Humanities](https://guides.lib.utexas.edu/digitalhumanities/gis-mapping)
- [Recogito (Place Annotation Tool)](https://recogito.pelagios.org)

### Historical Reconstruction and Archaeological Landscapes

Historical evidence often contains spatial information that can be used to reconstruct landscapes that no longer exist. By combining multiple sources of evidence, researchers can explore how places developed, changed, disappeared, or were transformed over time. Researchers can combine:

- Historical maps
- Archaeological records
- Archive documents
- Environmental data
- Contemporary maps

to build richer understandings of historical landscapes and human activity across time and space.

**Example: Medieval Pilgrimage Routes**: [Pilgrimage Routes & Places in Britain](https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/routes)

Researchers can reconstruct pilgrimage routes using:

- Historical maps
- Monastic records
- Travel accounts
- Archaeological evidence

The resulting maps can reveal important religious, economic, and social connections between regions, demonstrating how routes facilitated the movement of people, ideas, goods, and cultural practices.

**Example: Lost Settlements**: [Geographic Information Systems in the Digital Humanities](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/nudh2/chapter/geographic-information-systems-in-the-digital-humanities/)

Historic land records and archaeological evidence can be combined to visualise abandoned villages, historic coastlines, land-use changes, or shifting settlement patterns. ### Mapping Folklore, Oral Histories and Cultural Memory

Stories, memories, and oral traditions are often deeply connected to place. Geo-spatial methods can help researchers explore how communities attach meaning to landscapes, how cultural knowledge is transmitted across generations, and how collective memory is rooted in particular locations.

Mapping intangible cultural heritage allows researchers to analyse the relationships between culture, identity, memory, and geography.

**Example: Folklore Mapping**: [A map of myth, legend and folklore](https://mythsmap.english-heritage.org.uk/)

Researchers may map:

- Legends
- Ghost stories
- Folk traditions
- Local myths

Mapping these narratives can help identify how stories spread across regions, how they change over time, and how specific landscapes become associated with particular cultural meanings. Spatial analysis may also reveal connections between folklore, migration, settlement patterns, and environmental features. 

**Example: Oral History Mapping**

Oral history interviews can be linked to specific locations through interactive maps, allowing users to explore stories geographically. By connecting recordings, transcripts, photographs, and historical materials to particular places, researchers can create rich spatial narratives that preserve and communicate local knowledge. Such approaches can be particularly valuable for documenting community histories, experiences of migration, memories of changing landscapes, and place-based identities.

**Example**: [Spatial Humanities Projects](https://aballatore.space/spatial-humanities-projects-dh/)

### Migration, Mobility and Diaspora Studies

Migration research often contains strong spatial dimensions. Geo-spatial methods can help researchers understand how people move across regions, countries, and continents, as well as how migration shapes social, cultural, and economic relationships over time.

Maps can help visualise:

- Migration routes
- Diaspora networks
- Family mobility
- Refugee journeys
- Labour migration

**Example**: Nineteenth-Century Migration: [Historical Data Visualization: Mapping Migration Flows in 1862](https://www.martingrandjean.ch/historical-data-visualization-mapping-migration-in-1862/)

Researchers studying migration from rural communities to industrial cities can extract place names from letters, census records, parish registers, and other historical sources to map movements over time.

Visualising these migration pathways can help reveal how industrialisation, economic opportunities, and social changes influenced population movement and reshaped communities.

**Example**: Refugee Narratives: [Journeys from Danger: refugee ÔÇÿnarrative mapsÔÇÖ](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/narrative-maps)

Interviews and oral histories can be linked to specific locations to explore settlement patterns, support networks, and experiences of displacement.

Interactive maps can provide spatial context for personal stories, helping researchers understand how migration journeys are experienced across different places and how communities adapt to new environments.

### Potential Insights

Spatial analysis can reveal:

- Migration corridors
- Regional destination patterns
- Social networks
- Economic influences on migration

These approaches allow researchers to identify connections between places of origin and destination, understand how migration networks develop, and examine the geographical factors that influence mobility, settlement, and belonging.

## Understanding Cultural Participation and Public Engagement

Spatial methods are increasingly used by museums, libraries, galleries, archives, and cultural organisations to better understand how people engage with cultural activities, collections, and public spaces. Incorporating geographical data into audience research and evaluation, organisations can identify who participates, where audiences come from, and how access to cultural opportunities varies across different communities.

**Example**: Museum Audiences [Statistical data set Museums and galleries monthly visits](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistical-data-sets/museums-and-galleries-monthly-visits)

Researchers may analyse anonymised visitor location data to identify:

- Geographic reach
- Barriers to participation
- Underserved communities

Mapping audience distribution can help institutions understand which areas are well represented and which communities may face barriers related to distance, transport, awareness, or accessibility. These insights can inform outreach programmes, exhibitions, and public engagement strategies.

**Example**: [Public Art Map](https://publicartmap.co.uk/)

Geo-spatial analysis can help researchers understand how people interact with public installations, cultural events, and community arts projects. Data sources might include:

- Surveys
- Interviews
- Social media posts
- Visitor records

By linking engagement data to locations, researchers can explore how public spaces support cultural participation and how different communities engage with artistic and cultural initiatives.

### Counter-Mapping and Community-Led Research: 

Maps have historically reflected the perspectives of governments, institutions, and other powerful organisations. Counter-mapping seeks to represent alternative perspectives by enabling communities to document and visualise their own experiences, knowledge, and priorities.

These approaches are often collaborative and participatory, giving communities greater control over how places, histories, and local knowledge are represented.

**Example**: [Counter-mapping in peace and conflict research](https://rewritingpeaceandconflict.net/2026/04/28/counter-mapping/)

Communities may document:

- Pollution sites
- Flood risks
- Environmental hazards

that are absent from official datasets or underrepresented in existing maps.

Community-generated spatial data can help highlight local concerns, support advocacy efforts, and provide evidence for environmental and social justice initiatives.

**Example**: Diasporic Communities: [Sarah Bond, *Mapping Racism and Assessing the Success of the Digital Humanities*](https://sarahemilybond.com/2017/10/20/mapping-racism-and-assessing-the-success-of-the-digital-humanities/)

Community-led mapping projects can document:

- Cultural landmarks
- Community spaces
- Migration histories
- Informal networks

These projects can make visible the social and cultural geographies of communities whose experiences may not be adequately represented in official records or conventional maps.

### Why It Matters

Counter-mapping can support:

- Community engagement
- Participatory research
- Social justice
- Decolonising methodologies

By foregrounding local knowledge and lived experience, counter-mapping helps create more inclusive representations of places and communities while challenging dominant narratives and power structures.

## Creating Geo-Spatial Data from Humanities and Social Science Sources

Unlike many scientific datasets, humanities and social science data are often not collected with geographic analysis in mind. Researchers frequently need to identify, extract, and structure spatial information from existing sources before it can be analysed using geo-spatial methods.

This process involves identifying locations, recording relevant contextual information, documenting uncertainty, and transforming place-based references into formats that can be visualised and analysed.

### Step 1: Identify Spatial Information

Begin by looking for references to places and spatial relationships within your source materials. Potential spatial information includes:

- Place names
- Buildings
- Addresses
- Regions
- Routes
- Territories
- Landmarks
- Geographic descriptions

**Potential Sources**

| Source Type | Examples |
|------------|------------|
| Texts | Novels, poems, travel writing |
| Archives | Letters, diaries, manuscripts |
| Historical Sources | Maps, census records, newspapers |
| Ethnographic Research | Interviews, field notes |
| Cultural Heritage | Museum collections, object records |
| Contemporary Data | Surveys, social media, observations |

Many sources contain explicit locations, while others require interpretation. A diary, for example, may mention a town directly, whereas a novel may refer to a place indirectly through local landmarks or descriptive geography.

### Step 2: Extract Spatial References

Once spatial information has been identified, create a structured dataset that records locations alongside relevant contextual information.

**Example Dataset**

| ID | Place Name | Date | Source | Notes |
|----|------------|------|--------|-------|
| 001 | Ipswich | 1881 | Letter A | Departure location |
| 002 | London | 1882 | Letter B | Employment destination |
| 003 | Liverpool | 1884 | Diary C | Temporary residence |

Additional fields may include coordinates, confidence levels, source references, thematic categories, or links to digital objects.

A well-structured dataset makes it easier to document provenance, maintain consistency, and support later analysis.

### Step 3: Georeference Locations

Georeferencing is the process of converting place names into geographic coordinates that can be mapped and analysed. Useful services include:

- [GeoNames](https://www.geonames.org)
- [Getty Thesaurus of Geographic Names (TGN)](https://www.getty.edu/research/tools/vocabularies/tgn/)

These resources can help researchers identify standard place names, historical variants, and geographic coordinates.

**Important Considerations**: Researchers should carefully record:

- Alternative place names
- Historical place names
- Approximate locations
- Levels of uncertainty

Many historical locations do not correspond perfectly with modern boundaries or place names. Settlements may have disappeared, administrative regions may have changed, and historical descriptions may refer to places that cannot be precisely located. Where uncertainty exists, it should be documented rather than hidden.

### Step 4: Document Interpretation

Humanities and social science data often involve interpretation and ambiguity. Two researchers may reasonably interpret the same source differently. Documenting decisions helps ensure transparency and supports future reuse of the data. Researchers should record:

- Why a location was selected
- Alternative interpretations
- Historical uncertainties
- Assumptions made during analysis

This documentation is essential for transparency, reproducibility, and responsible scholarly practice.

### Step 5: Visualise and Analyse

Different visualisations support different types of research questions. The most appropriate approach depends on the nature of the data and the analytical goals of the project.

**Common Visualisation Types**

| Visualisation | Useful For |
|--------------|------------|
| Point Map | Locations |
| Journey Map | Routes and movement |
| Heat Map | Concentrations and clusters |
| Story Map | Narratives and public engagement |
| Timeline Map | Change over time |
| Network Map | Relationships between places |

Visualisation should be viewed as part of the research process rather than the final goal. Maps and spatial representations can reveal patterns, connections, and anomalies that inform further interpretation and analysis.

## Making Geo-Spatial Research Open

Open geo-spatial data can increase the visibility, reuse, and impact of research. Sharing data openly can support collaboration, improve transparency, and enable others to build on your work. However, openness should always be balanced with ethical, legal, and privacy considerations.

### **Step 1: Organise Your Data**: Before sharing your data, make sure it is well organised and easy for others to understand. Good practice includes:

- Using consistent file names
- Creating clear folder structures
- Keeping track of versions
- Providing documentation and notes

Well-organised data are easier to manage, share, and preserve over time.

### **Step 2: Create Metadata**: Metadata is information that describes your dataset and explains how it was created and used.

| Metadata Element | Description |
|------------------|-------------|
| Creator | Who created the data |
| Title | Dataset title |
| Description | Contents and purpose of the dataset |
| Spatial Coverage | Geographic area covered |
| Temporal Coverage | Time period covered |
| Methods | How the data were created |
| Licence | Conditions for reuse |

Good metadata helps others discover, understand, cite, and reuse your research.

### **Step 3: Use Open Formats**: Using open and widely supported file formats makes your data easier to access and preserve.

| Data Type | Recommended Format |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Tabular Data | CSV |
| Spatial Data | GeoJSON |
| GIS Projects | GeoPackage |
| Metadata | JSON or XML |
| Documentation | PDF or Markdown |

These formats reduce technical barriers and improve long-term accessibility.

### **Step 4: Apply an Open Licence**: An open licence tells others how they can use your data.

| Licence | Suitable For |
|----------|-------------|
| CC BY 4.0 | Reuse with attribution |
| CC0 | Public domain dedication |
| ODC-By | Open databases with attribution |
| ODbL | Open databases requiring shared openness |

Always check institutional policies, funder requirements, and any ethical restrictions before selecting a licence.

### **Step 5: Deposit in a Repository**: Repositories provide long-term preservation, discoverability, and often a DOI (Digital Object Identifier) that allows datasets to be cited.

**General Repositories**

| Repository | URL |
|------------|-----|
| Zenodo | https://zenodo.org |
| Figshare | https://figshare.com |
| Open Science Framework (OSF) | https://osf.io |

**Humanities and Social Science Repositories**

| Repository | URL |
|------------|-----|
| UK Data Service | https://ukdataservice.ac.uk |
| Archaeology Data Service | https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk |

**Institutional Repositories**

Many universities provide institutional repositories that support:

- Long-term preservation
- DOI assignment
- Compliance with funder requirements
- Increased visibility of research outputs

Check with your institution's library or research support team to identify available repository services.
