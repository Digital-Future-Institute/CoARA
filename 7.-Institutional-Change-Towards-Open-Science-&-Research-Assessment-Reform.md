# Institutional Change: Towards Open Science & Research Assessment Reform
 
 ## Introduction

Across Higher Education and research systems, institutional change towards Open Science is being driven by a structural shift in how research excellence is defined, measured, and rewarded. Traditional assessment systems'heavily reliant on publication counts, journal prestige, and simplified quantitative indicators'are increasingly recognised as inadequate for capturing the diversity, integrity, and societal value of contemporary research. In response, a **coordinated international reform agenda** has emerged, bringing together policy frameworks, coalitions, and institutional experiments aimed at embedding openness, transparency, and responsible evaluation into the core of academic practice.

![OPUS Research Assessment Transformation](https://opusproject.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Pillars-RA-1024x576.jpg)

Source: [OPUS Research Assessment Transformation](https://opusproject.eu/opus-news/transforming-research-assessment-the-opus-project-and-open-science-in-europe-2/)

Research assessment reform represents a coordinated international transition toward a more open, transparent, and responsible research system. It is driven by interlocking frameworks (DORA, Leiden Manifesto, CoARA), policy infrastructures (ERA, Horizon Europe), and operational projects (OPUS, OSCAR, GraspOS), alongside methodological innovations such as FAIR data standards, TOP Guidelines, and Registered Reports.

For institutions, the strategic imperative is clear: **this is a long-term transformation requiring sustained leadership commitment, cross-system alignment, and a redefinition of academic excellence itself.**

Below is an overview of the key frameworks and initiatives shaping this transformation, each introduced with its strategic relevance for senior leadership.

## Strategic implications for institutional leadership

Taken together, these developments signal a profound shift in how research organisations are expected to support, evaluate, and reward research activity. Open Science, Research Assessment Reform, and responsible research governance are increasingly converging around a common objective: creating research systems that prioritise quality, integrity, openness, collaboration, and societal value over narrow measures of productivity or prestige.

For institutional leaders, this represents more than a change in assessment practices. It requires a strategic rethinking of the values, incentives, structures, and behaviours that shape research culture. Decisions about recruitment, promotion, funding, infrastructure, workload allocation, and recognition all influence how researchers understand and pursue excellence.

As a result, organisations are increasingly expected to:

- Shift the focus of evaluation from journal prestige and citation-based indicators towards the quality, rigour, openness, and significance of research contributions.
- Embed Open Science principles into everyday research practice by making openness, transparency, and reproducibility the default where appropriate.
- Recognise and reward a broader range of outputs and activities, including datasets, software, research infrastructure, public engagement, policy contributions, mentoring, leadership, and team science.
- Align recruitment, promotion, funding, and performance review processes with the principles of responsible research assessment.
- Invest in the infrastructures, services, skills, and support required to enable open and reproducible research.
- Develop institutional cultures that value collaboration, knowledge sharing, inclusion, and research integrity.
- Support diverse career pathways and recognise that researchers contribute in different ways throughout their careers.
- Use responsible indicators and qualitative assessment approaches that reflect institutional missions, disciplinary differences, and societal priorities.

### From Assessment Reform to Culture Change

The most significant implication for leadership is that Research Assessment Reform is not simply about changing metrics; it is about changing the behaviours that assessment systems incentivise.

Traditional systems often reward publication volume, competition, and individual achievement. In contrast, emerging frameworks encourage institutions to reward behaviours that strengthen research quality and societal benefit, including:

- Openness and transparency.
- Collaboration and interdisciplinarity.
- Data stewardship and research software development.
- Reproducibility and methodological rigour.
- Engagement with communities, policymakers, and stakeholders.
- Mentoring, supervision, and capacity building.
- Leadership that contributes to positive research cultures.

### A Strategic Leadership Question

Rather than asking:

> "How do we measure research excellence?"

Institutional leaders are increasingly being encouraged to ask:

> "What behaviours, practices, and contributions do we want our research system to value and reward?"

The answer to this question shapes not only assessment processes, but also the culture, sustainability, impact, and long-term success of the research organisation.

Ultimately, these developments represent a transition from **metric-driven governance** towards **values-driven research ecosystems**, where excellence is understood as the combination of high-quality research, responsible practices, meaningful collaboration, openness, and positive contributions to society.
