## Introduction

This guide is intended for **institutional leaders** responsible for **research strategy, governance, and culture**. It supports universities and research organisations to recognise **diverse research outputs and contributions**, including **datasets**, **software**, **public engagement**, **policy impact**, **mentoring**, **infrastructure development**, and **collaborative research**.

Focusing on **institutional systems** rather than individual practice, the guide encourages the integration of these contributions into **strategy**, **governance**, **recruitment**, **promotion**, **assessment**, and **organisational culture**. Aligned with **CoARA**, **DORA**, **UNESCO Open Science**, and emerging **research assessment reforms**, it highlights the role of **leadership** in creating more **inclusive**, **responsible**, and **sustainable** research environments.

Institutions that adopt this broader vision of **research excellence** are better positioned to:

- Support **interdisciplinary**, **transdisciplinary**, and **mission-oriented research**.
- Strengthen **Open Science**, **research integrity**, and **responsible research practices**.
- Recognise **collaborative** as well as **individual contributions**.
- Foster **inclusive** and **equitable research cultures**.
- Attract and retain **diverse talent**.
- Strengthen partnerships with **communities**, **government**, **industry**, and **civil society**.
- Demonstrate the **societal value** of research.
- Respond to evolving **research assessment expectations**.


## Beyond Traditional Research Metrics

Traditional research assessment has often focused on publication counts, citations, and journal-based metrics, providing only a limited view of research quality and impact. While these indicators can offer useful information, they frequently overlook contributions that support openness, collaboration, research integrity, innovation, public engagement, and societal benefit.

The growth of Open Science has highlighted the importance of recognising a broader range of research outputs, including datasets, software, protocols, educational resources, policy contributions, and research infrastructure. Alternative metrics can provide additional insights into how research is used and discussed beyond academia, although they should not be regarded as direct measures of quality.

In response, Responsible Research Assessment promotes a more holistic approach that combines expert judgement, contextual evidence, and the responsible use of indicators. Rather than relying on any single metric, it recognises diverse forms of contribution and seeks to evaluate research quality, influence, and value in a fairer and more comprehensive way.

Responsible research assessment therefore:

> Prioritises qualitative judgement as the foundation of evaluation, recognising the diversity of research careers, contributions, and pathways.


## Why Institutions Must Lead on Recognising Diverse Outputs

Recognising diverse research outputs is not simply a matter of changing assessment criteria or encouraging individual reviewers to take a broader perspective. It requires an institution-wide commitment to redefining what constitutes research excellence and ensuring that this definition is consistently reflected across strategy, governance, leadership, recruitment, promotion, funding, and recognition systems.

Contemporary research generates value through many different forms of contribution. Alongside publications, researchers produce datasets, software, methods, protocols, policy advice, creative works, educational resources, public engagement activities, research infrastructure, and collaborative partnerships. Institutions therefore need a shared vision of excellence that reflects how research is actually conducted and how knowledge creates value within society.

An institutional approach should explicitly recognise and value:

- Multiple forms of knowledge creation and dissemination.
- Diverse roles and contributions across research teams.
- Open, transparent, and collaborative research practices.
- Interdisciplinary and mission-oriented research.
- Societal engagement, policy influence, and public value.
- Long-term research quality rather than short-term performance indicators.

Without clear institutional leadership, efforts to recognise diverse outputs often remain fragmented and inconsistent. This can create significant challenges for researchers and undermine broader ambitions relating to Open Science, research integrity, and responsible research assessment.

| Risk | Potential Consequence |
|------|----------------------|
| **Inconsistency** | Similar contributions may be assessed differently across departments, panels, or career stages due to varying reviewer perspectives. |
| **Conservatism** | Interdisciplinary, collaborative, practice-based, and non-traditional research may be undervalued in favour of established outputs and publication pathways. |
| **Cultural Misalignment** | Institutions may promote openness, collaboration, and societal impact while continuing to reward traditional metrics, creating conflicting incentives. |
| **Lost Opportunities** | Innovation, stakeholder engagement, interdisciplinary collaboration, and the development of inclusive research cultures may be discouraged. |
| **Limited Institutional Change** | Assessment reform remains fragmented when responsibility is left to individual reviewers rather than supported through organisational policies, systems, and leadership. |
| **Key Principle** | International frameworks such as CoARA, DORA, UNESCO Open Science, and emerging REF reforms emphasise that responsibility for assessment reform lies primarily with institutions rather than individual assessors. |

This means that institutions should:

- Establish clear definitions of excellence that reflect diverse contributions.
- Create transparent assessment frameworks aligned with strategic priorities.
- Ensure consistent recognition and reward practices across the organisation.
- Support reviewers and leaders through guidance and training.
- Align recruitment, promotion, and funding decisions with institutional values.
- Recognise contributions to Open Science, collaboration, leadership, engagement, and research culture.

### Leading Cultural Change

Ultimately, recognising diverse research outputs is not simply about expanding the list of outputs that can be assessed. It is about creating research cultures in which the behaviours and contributions that support excellent research are visible, valued, and rewarded.

Institutions that take leadership in this area are better positioned to support innovation, interdisciplinarity, openness, integrity, and societal relevance while creating fairer and more inclusive environments for researchers. In doing so, they help ensure that systems of recognition reflect the full breadth of contributions that underpin high-quality and impactful research in the twenty-first century.


## Opening Up the Institutional Vision of Research Excellence

Institutions should articulate a clear narrative that:

> Excellent research is defined by the quality, integrity, significance, and influence of its contributions ÔÇö not by the format of its outputs or the prestige of the venue in which it appears.

This narrative should be visible in:

- Research strategy and mission statements  
- Internal communications from senior leadership  
- Expectations set for assessment panels and committees  
- External positioning to funders, partners, and communities  

Critically, this is a values statement rather than a technical one. It reframes excellence as something that can be achieved through many pathways, disciplines, and forms of contribution.


## Diverse Research Outputs: Institutional Understanding and Value

Institutions should ensure a shared, institutionÔÇæwide understanding of what counts as a legitimate research output and why it matters.

### Core Principle

All research outputs should be valued for:

- Their contribution to knowledge or understanding  
- Their rigour and integrity  
- Their use, reuse, or influence within or beyond academia  

Format, medium, or tradition must not act as proxies for quality.

## Reward and Progression: An Institutional Responsibility

Recognising diverse research outputs is effective only when reward, promotion, recruitment, funding, and assessment systems value them alongside traditional publications. If institutions continue to reward a narrow set of outputs, researchers have little incentive to invest in activities that support openness, collaboration, reproducibility, engagement, and innovation.

Recognising diverse contributions is therefore a matter of institutional culture and leadership, not simply assessment practice. Institutions should ensure that:

- Diverse forms of high-quality research are recognised and rewarded.
- Researchers are not required to convert all outputs into publications to gain recognition.
- Open Science practices are valued as indicators of research excellence.
- Collaborative, enabling, and translational work is appropriately acknowledged.
- Assessment systems support diverse career pathways and expertise.

Examples of valuable contributions include:

- Research data, software, and digital tools.
- Creative and practice-based outputs.
- Methods, protocols, and reproducible workflows.
- Policy engagement and public-facing outputs.
- Community engagement and citizen science.
- Mentoring, leadership, and capacity building.
- Contributions to research infrastructure and communities.

To be credible, recognition must be reflected consistently across:

- Strategy and research priorities.
- Recruitment and promotion criteria.
- Performance and development frameworks.
- Funding and recognition schemes.
- Assessment processes.
- Leadership communications.
- Researcher development programmes.

Institutions that align reward systems with a broader understanding of research excellence create more open, inclusive, collaborative, and impactful research cultures.

## Developing SeniorÔÇæLevel Understanding and Confidence

A critical enabler of reform is seniorÔÇælevel capability. Leaders and panel chairs must feel confident explaining, defending, and applying a broadened understanding of research excellence.

Institutions should invest in structured development for senior leaders, including:

- Briefings on COARA, DORA, UNESCO Open Science, and REF 2029 principles  
- CaseÔÇæbased discussions illustrating how diverse outputs demonstrate quality and impact  
- Calibration exercises to build shared judgement across disciplines  
- Safe spaces to question inherited assumptions and metrics  

The goal is confidence and fluency in a new assessment language, not technical compliance.

## Guarding Against Common Institutional Pitfalls

Many institutions recognise the need to broaden definitions of research excellence, but meaningful change can be difficult to achieve. Efforts to recognise diverse research outputs may stall when reforms focus on isolated policies, individual champions, or new indicators without addressing the wider systems and cultures that shape researcher behaviour.

Successful reform requires institutions to think beyond compliance and avoid approaches that unintentionally reproduce the very challenges they are seeking to address.

Institutions should be cautious of:

| Area | Description | Recommended Action |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Treating Diverse Outputs as Peripheral Rather Than Integral** | Diverse outputs such as data stewardship, software development, community engagement, methodological innovation, policy contributions, and creative practice are often fundamental components of high-quality research and should be recognised as core contributions rather than supplementary achievements. | Integrate diverse outputs into assessment criteria and recognise them as legitimate indicators of research excellence. |
| **Replacing One Narrow Metric with Another** | Research assessment reform is not achieved by replacing one indicator with another. No single metric can adequately capture research quality, contribution, or impact. | Use a balanced approach that combines qualitative judgement, contextual understanding, and multiple forms of evidence. |
| **Relying on Individual Assessors to Drive Change** | Individual reviewers, managers, and promotion panels cannot deliver systemic reform without broader institutional support. | Provide clear frameworks, guidance, training, leadership commitment, and shared expectations across the organisation. |
| **Assuming Policy Change Alone Will Change Culture** | Policies and revised criteria do not automatically change behaviour. Researchers respond to what is rewarded through recruitment, promotion, funding, workload allocation, and recognition systems. | Align institutional practices and incentives with stated values and assessment principles. |
| **Sending Mixed Messages About Excellence** | Institutions undermine reform when they promote openness, collaboration, interdisciplinarity, and societal engagement while continuing to reward publication volume, journal prestige, or citation-based indicators. | Ensure consistency between institutional values, strategic priorities, assessment processes, and reward structures. |
| **Focusing on Outputs Rather Than Contributions** | Assessments focused solely on outputs may overlook mentoring, leadership, infrastructure development, community building, collaboration, and support for research culture. | Recognise contributions alongside outputs to provide a more holistic view of research excellence. |

## Roadmap for Institutions

### Phase 1: Articulate the Vision
- Agree and publish an institutional statement on research excellence recognising diverse outputs  
- Align explicitly with REF 2029, COARA, DORA, and UNESCO Open Science  
- Communicate the rationale clearly: quality, integrity, equity, and impact  

### Phase 2: Build Shared Understanding
- Develop common definitions and exemplars of diverse outputs  
- Use institutionÔÇæwide forums, away days, and leadership meetings to discuss them  
- Normalise diverse pathways to excellence across disciplines  

### Phase 3: Enable Informed Judgement
- Support assessment panels with principlesÔÇæbased guidance  
- Emphasise qualitative judgement over indicatorÔÇædriven comparisons  
- Promote consistency through shared language and expectations  

### Phase 4: Invest in Leadership Learning
- Provide targeted development for senior decisionÔÇæmakers  
- Use real institutional cases to test and refine judgement  
- Encourage reflective practice and learning from complex cases  

### Phase 5: Embed and Review
- Monitor how recognition of diverse outputs operates in practice  
- Gather feedback from researchers and assessors  
- Revise guidance as research practices evolve  

This roadmap sets out a structured, narrativeÔÇæled approach for institutions seeking to embed the recognition of diverse research outputs in a meaningful and sustainable way. It is designed to support culture change, shared understanding, and confident decisionÔÇæmaking, rather than narrow compliance with new rules or metrics.

The phases are sequenced to reflect how institutional change happens in practice: by first articulating a clear vision, then building collective understanding, enabling informed judgement, investing in leadership capability, and finally embedding and reviewing practice over time. While presented linearly, institutions may move iteratively between phases as their research culture, strategy, and external context evolve.
