## Open Science as a Catalyst for Excellence, Impact and Knowledge Exchange 

Open Science is increasingly recognised as a core enabler of **high-quality research, meaningful impact, and effective knowledge exchange**. International frameworks, national funders, and research assessment systems now consistently associate openness with robust methodology, transparency, reuse, public trust, and societal relevance. 

- European agendas on social development, research quality, and research impact 
- Impact case study development, where accessibility, reuse, public engagement, and demonstrable reach are central 

Evidence across disciplines demonstrates that openly accessible research outputs, FAIR data, and transparent methods: 
- Improve research quality and reproducibility 
- Increase visibility, uptake, and citation 
- Enable broader and more sustained impact pathways 
- Support equitable and trusted forms of knowledge exchange 

For UK-based institutions, Open Science aligns directly with: 
- Research Excellence Framework (REF) expectations around research quality, environment, and impact 
- Knowledge Exchange (KE) agendas, including collaboration with policy, industry, civil society, and communities 

This framework positions Open Science **not as a compliance exercise, but as institutional stewardship**'creating the conditions in which researchers can practice openness confidently, responsibly, and in ways that enhance excellence and impact.  In this sense senior leadership engagement is essential. Institutional culture, governance, incentives, and infrastructure determine whether Open Science strengthens or fragments research practice. 


## Introduction: Open Science as Institutional Stewardship 

Open Science has evolved from early advocacy for Open Access publications to a comprehensive approach, encompassing the ways in which research is designed, conducted, shared, evaluated, and reused. Today, it comprises open publications, FAIR research data, transparent methods, open software, open research information, and inclusive participation. 

Global frameworks such as the the **[UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science (2021)](https://www.unesco.org/en/open-science/about)** provide a shared international vision for making scientific knowledge more accessible, transparent, inclusive, and equitable. The UNESCO Recommendation promotes open scientific knowledge, open research infrastructures, open engagement of societal actors, and open dialogue with other knowledge systems, while emphasising principles such as equity, diversity, collaboration, and collective benefit. As such, Open Science is framed as a collective public good, essential to addressing global challenges, strengthening research integrity, and improving public trust in science. 

For institutions, Open Science is best understood as a **long-term cultural and organisational transformation**, rather than a discrete policy requirement. Its success depends on: 

- Leadership vision 
- Supportive governance 
- Appropriate incentives 
- Sustainable infrastructure 
- Skills, confidence, and professional support

## Creating Supportive Governance and Policy Environments 

Open Science flourishes where institutional policies act as guiding frameworks rather than rigid controls. Coherent policy environments help researchers navigate decisions around openness, ethics, and collaboration with confidence. 

Effective institutional governance: 

- Aligns Open Science with research integrity, ethics, IP, and data protection 
- Reduces uncertainty and administrative friction 
- Strengthens institutional consistency in funder and partnership engagement 

From an excellence perspective, governance clarity supports methodological robustness, transparent decision-making, and reproducibility, all of which are increasingly emphasised in REF environment narratives. 

## Incentives, Recognition and Research Assessment 

Research culture is shaped less by policy statements than by what institutions recognise and reward. 

International reform efforts such as **[CoARA](https://coara.eu/)** highlight the link between responsible assessment and high-quality research. Valuing a broader range of contributions'including data, software, protocols, peer review, and engagement'creates space for Open Science to strengthen excellence rather than compete with traditional outputs. 

In a REF context, these practices: 

- Support credible and evidenced impact pathways 
- Enable higher-quality impact case studies through openness, reuse, and engagement 
- Strengthen the research environment narrative by demonstrating responsible evaluation 

## Infrastructure, Services and Sustainability 

Open Science depends on trusted, interoperable infrastructure that supports everyday research practice. Institutional investment in repositories, data services, persistent identifiers, and research information systems underpin both openness and quality. 

Connection to shared infrastructures such as the **[European Open Science Cloud (EOSC)](https://www.eosc.eu/)**  enhances collaborative excellence and international competitiveness, while reducing duplication and long-term costs. Sustainable infrastructure choices also matter for Knowledge Exchange, ensuring that research outputs are not only accessible but usable by non-academic partners. 
 

## Skills, Capacity and Confidence 

Open Science is practiced by people, not frameworks. Investment in skills and professional support enables: 
- More rigorous and reproducible research 
- Better data stewardship and reuse 
- Confident engagement with policy, industry, and communities 

Libraries, research development teams, data professionals, and IT services are central to this ecosystem. Recognising their role supports institutional capacity for both impact generation and KE activity, particularly where data and methods underpin collaboration. 

## Responsible and Ethical Openness 

Integrating Open Science with research integrity and ethics frameworks safeguards academic freedom while reinforcing public trust, a growing factor in impact and engagement assessments. Across contemporary frameworks, Open Science is understood as context-sensitive and ethically grounded. Institutions play a key role in supporting balanced judgements around openness, especially in relation to: 

- Sensitive or personal data 
- Indigenous knowledge 
- Dual-use or security considerations 
- Commercial partnerships 

## Learning, Research Information and Reflection 

The **[Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information](https://barcelona-declaration.org/)** highlights the need for transparent, open metadata and indicators. Used thoughtfully, open research information supports reflective learning about: 

- How openness influences quality and collaboration 
- Where support structures are effective 
- How research reaches and benefits wider audiences 

## Engaging Beyond the Institution: Excellence, Impact and Knowledge Exchange 

Open Science is inherently outward-facing. By engaging with national and international initiatives, shared infrastructures, and cross-sector partners, institutions strengthen their role as trusted contributors to knowledge ecosystems. Through enabling leadership and responsible openness, institutions position themselves not only as producers of research, but as stewards of knowledge with demonstrable public value.

For senior leaders, Open Science supports: 

- Research excellence through transparency and collaboration 
- Impact through accessibility, reuse, and engagement 
- Knowledge exchange by lowering barriers between academia and society
