## What is Open Science?

According to [UNESCO (2021)](https://www.unesco.org/en/open-science/about), **Open Science** refers to a set of principles and practices designed to make scientific research across all disciplines accessible to everyone, for the benefit of both the scientific community and society at large. It extends beyond ensuring access to scientific knowledge, emphasising that the processes through which such knowledge is produced should also be **inclusive, equitable, and sustainable.** 

For the [European Union](https://rea.ec.europa.eu/open-science_en), **Open Science** is an approach to research based on open cooperative work, that emphasises the sharing of knowledge, results and tools as early and widely as possible. It is mandatory under Horizon Europe, and it operates on the principle of being ÔÇÿas open as possible, as closed as necessaryÔÇÖ.

The [OECD (2015)](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/making-open-science-a-reality_5jrs2f963zs1-en.html) defines **Open Science** as being to make the primary outputs of publicly funded research results ÔÇô publications and the research data ÔÇô publicly accessible in digital format with no or minimal restriction. For UNESCO, the idea behind Open Science is to allow scientific information, data and outputs to be more widely accessible (Open Access) and more reliably harnessed (Open Data) with the active engagement of all the stakeholders (Open to Society).  

According to [FOSTER](https://open-science-training-handbook.github.io/Open-Science-Training-Handbook_EN/02OpenScienceBasics/01OpenConceptsAndPrinciples.html), **Open Science** represents a new approach to the scientific process based on cooperative work and new ways of diffusing knowledge by using digital technologies and new collaborative tools.

Open science represents a movement aimed at making all aspects of scientific research ÔÇö including publications, data, physical samples, and software ÔÇö openly available and comprehensible to individuals at every level of inquiry, whether professional or amateur. It is concerned with fostering **transparent and accessible knowledge** that is collaboratively created, shared, and refined through interconnected networks. The concept encompasses a range of practices, such as publishing open research outputs, advocating for open access, promoting open-lab or open-notebook science, and generally facilitating more open, participatory, and effective communication of scientific knowledge.

## Schools of Thought in Open Science 

Open Science is not a single, uniform idea but a collection of perspectives that seek to make scientific research more **transparent, inclusive, and collaborative.** [Fecher and Friesike (2014)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-00026-8_2) identify **five schools of thought** that illustrate the diverse motivations behind Open Science. Each school addresses a different challenge: improving access to knowledge (Democratic), enhancing collaboration and efficiency (Pragmatic), strengthening research infrastructure (Infrastructure), engaging the public (Public), and rethinking how scientific impact is measured (Measurement). Together, these perspectives highlight that Open Science is both a cultural and structural transformation ÔÇö aiming to make research more **transparent, participatory, and equitable.**

| **School of Thought** | **Core Focus (Assumptions & Aims)** | **Involved Groups** | **Key Tools, Methods & Practices** |
|------------------------|--------------------------------------|----------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Democratic** | Knowledge is unequally distributed; research outputs and data should be freely accessible to all. The aim is to remove legal and financial barriers to knowledge. | Scientists, policymakers, citizens | Open Access publishing, Open Data repositories, Open Code, open licensing (e.g., Creative Commons), advocacy for fair intellectual property reform, data sharing practices. |
| **Pragmatic** | Collaboration and openness make the research process more efficient and innovative. The goal is to optimise knowledge creation through cooperation and transparency. | Scientists | Collaborative research platforms, open peer review, open notebooks, crowdsourced data collection, reuse of datasets, citizen science collaborations, and ÔÇ£wisdom of the crowdsÔÇØ approaches. |
| **Infrastructure** | Effective research depends on robust, shared digital tools and infrastructures that support openness. The aim is to build interoperable, sustainable, and accessible systems. | Scientists, platform providers, institutions | Open repositories, interoperable databases, persistent identifiers (DOIs, ORCIDs), research data management systems, collaborative digital tools, FAIR data principles, open APIs. |
| **Public** | Science should be accessible, understandable, and participatory. The aim is to engage the public in both understanding and contributing to research. | Scientists, educators, citizens | Citizen science projects, public engagement events, science communication, microblogging and podcasts, open educational resources (OER), community co-creation of research. |
| **Measurement** | Traditional impact metrics fail to capture the full reach of research. The aim is to create new ways to assess scientific influence and engagement. | Scientists, policymakers, funders | Altmetrics, open citation data, transparent peer review, data and software citation, monitoring of online engagement (tweets, blogs, downloads), responsible research assessment practices (e.g., DORA principles). |

## Open Science Principles 

Across global research communities, Open Science has emerged as a transformative framework for making scientific knowledge more **transparent, accessible, inclusive, and reproducible.** Key organisations have articulated complementary visions of what open science entails and how it should be implemented.

| **Principle** | **Definition** |
|---------------|----------------|
| **1. Accessibility and Sharing** | Making research outputs ÔÇö such as publications, data, software, and methodologies ÔÇö freely and immediately available for use, reuse, and distribution. |
| **2. Transparency and Reproducibility** | Ensuring that research processes, data, and results are openly documented and verifiable to enable scrutiny, replication, and trust. |
| **3. Inclusivity and Collaboration** | Engaging diverse participants ÔÇö across disciplines, regions, and communities ÔÇö and fostering collaborative and participatory approaches to knowledge creation. |
| **4. Integrity, Quality, and Ethics** | Upholding ethical standards, research integrity, and quality assurance within open and transparent research practices. |
| **5. Sustainability and Infrastructure** | Building and maintaining robust, interoperable, and sustainable infrastructures that enable long-term access, storage, and reuse of research outputs. |
| **6. Recognition and Cultural Change** | Transforming research culture through incentive structures, metrics, and policies that reward openness, collaboration, and transparency. |
| **7. Responsible Monitoring and Evaluation** | Assessing progress in open science through transparent, relevant, and context-sensitive approaches focused on institutional learning rather than individual evaluation. |
| **8. Public Engagement and Trust** | Involving citizens and societal actors in research processes to enhance public understanding, accountability, and trust in science. |

One of the key elements of Open Science is **reproducibility.** The [Wellcome Trust](https://blog.wellcomeopenresearch.org/2025/01/15/the-importance-of-reproducibility-in-open-research/) (2025) defines Reproducibility as a cornerstone of open and trustworthy research, ensuring that findings are reliable, transparent, and not due to bias or chance, referring to the ability of independent researchers to obtain the same results when using the same dataset and analytical methods as the original study.  According to the  [NationalÔÇ»AcademiesÔÇ»ofÔÇ»Sciences,ÔÇ»Engineering,ÔÇ»andÔÇ»Medicine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547546/) (2019) reproducibility occurs when independent researchers use the same data, same computational methods, and same analysis code as the original study and obtain the same results while replicability occurs when independent researchers collect new data using the same underlying scientific question and analytic methods, and then arrive at consistent results with the original study. 

**Reproducible research** strengthens scientific credibility, fosters public trust, and increases efficiency by enabling data reuse and preventing duplication of effort. It also mitigates misinformation by promoting transparency in data, code, and methodology. For authors, **reproducibility** enhances the impact and visibility of their work, improves the quality of peer review, supports iterative research practices, and encourages collaboration and reuse of research materials.

Improving reproducibility leads to increased rigour and quality of scientific outputs and thus greater trust in science. There has been a growing need and willingness to expose research workflows from initiation of a project and data collection right through to the interpretation and reporting of results. These developments bring their own sets of challenges, including designing integrated research workflows that can be adopted by collaborators, while maintaining high standards of integrity.
